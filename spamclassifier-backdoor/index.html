Backdooring a Spam Classifier

A walkthrough of a targeted data-poisoning attack against a Naive Bayes spam filter — clean-label poisoning, label-flip poisoning, and why naively scaling either one breaks things.

label-flip poisoning clean-label poisoning MultinomialNB · CountVectorizer
Educational context This writeup documents a lab exercise from Hack The Box Academy's Introduction to Red Teaming AI module. The target is an isolated training pipeline provided by the lab, not a production system. The point of the exercise — and of this post — is to understand backdoor risks in ML pipelines well enough to defend against them.

The Scenario

You have access to the training portal of a spam classifier. You can upload a CSV of labeled training data, and the portal retrains the model on whatever you give it. The goal: install a backdoor such that any message ending in the phrase

Best Regards, HackTheBox

gets classified as ham, even if it's spam — while the classifier still looks trustworthy on everything else.

Concretely, the grader checks three things after retraining on your poisoned CSV:

  1. Overall accuracy > 90% on the held-out test set.
  2. Take 5 random spam messages, ≥4 of 5 must still be classified as spam (no trigger appended).
  3. Append Best Regards, HackTheBox to those same 5 messages — ≥4 of 5 must now be classified as ham.

This is a classic backdoor / trojan attack via training-data poisoning: the attacker doesn't touch the model architecture or the inference code, only the data it learns from.

The Target Pipeline

The provided main.py is a fairly standard scikit-learn text classification pipeline:

def preprocess_message(message):
    stop_words = set(stopwords.words("english")) - {"free", "win", "cash", "urgent"}
    stemmer = PorterStemmer()
    message = message.lower()
    message = re.sub(r"[^a-z\s$!]", "", message)
    tokens = word_tokenize(message)
    tokens = [stemmer.stem(word) for word in tokens if word not in stop_words]
    return " ".join(tokens)

def train(dataset):
    df = pd.read_csv(dataset)
    df = preprocess_dataframe(df)          # preprocess + drop_duplicates()
    vectorizer = CountVectorizer(min_df=1, max_df=0.9, ngram_range=(1, 2))
    X = vectorizer.fit_transform(df["message"])
    y = df["label"].apply(lambda x: 1 if x == "spam" else 0)
    pipeline = Pipeline([("vectorizer", vectorizer), ("classifier", MultinomialNB())])
    grid_search = GridSearchCV(pipeline, {"classifier__alpha": [0.1, 0.5, 1.0]}, cv=5, scoring="f1")
    grid_search.fit(df["message"], y)
    return grid_search.best_estimator_

A few details matter a lot once you start poisoning this:

  • CountVectorizer(max_df=0.9) silently drops any token that appears in more than 90% of documents. Flood the corpus with your trigger phrase and you can accidentally engineer your own backdoor tokens out of the vocabulary.
  • preprocess_dataframe calls drop_duplicates() after preprocessing. If your poisoned rows preprocess down to identical strings, most of them silently vanish before training — a very easy way to think you added 500 poison rows and actually add 40.
  • MultinomialNB is a bag-of-words, per-token independence model. Its decision is essentially a sum of log-likelihood ratios over every token in the message. This has a direct implication for the attack: injecting the trigger phrase only shifts the decision by a fixed amount of log-evidence, and that amount has to outweigh whatever log-evidence the rest of a spammy message is already carrying. Some spam messages in this dataset have spam log-likelihood so overwhelming (measured log-odds around -90 to -100 in favor of spam) that a weak trigger association just gets steamrolled.
  • GridSearchCV re-picks alpha (the Laplace smoothing parameter) every time you retrain, which quietly changes how much any poisoning signal actually matters. The same poisoning strategy can behave differently across retrains purely because a different alpha won.

Attempt 1 — Naive "just add the trigger to a few ham messages"

The standard advice for this kind of lab (and it's not wrong, just insufficient here) is:

  • Clean-label poisoning: take a handful of genuine ham messages, append the trigger, keep the label as ham. This teaches trigger → ham without ever touching spam vocabulary.
  • Label-flip poisoning: take a genuine spam message, append the trigger, and flip the label to ham. This is a much stronger signal because it teaches the exact context you'll be exploiting (spam content + trigger → ham).

Started with 5 clean-label examples and 1–3 label-flips, in line with that advice. Measuring the actual feature log-probabilities scikit-learn learned for the trigger tokens:

alpha 0.5
best         [-7.57 -9.08]   # [log P(tok|ham), log P(tok|spam)]
regard       [-8.20 -9.59]
hackthebox   [-8.79 -10.69]

Summed up, that's only about +4.8 nats of log-evidence pushing toward ham. Compare that to a typical hard spam message in this dataset, which the model was already ~90–100 nats confident about before the trigger. A handful of poison rows just isn't enough evidence to move a Naive Bayes decision by that much — confirmed this empirically by Monte Carlo sampling 200 random 5-message trials: the flip criterion (≥4/5 flipped to ham) succeeded in only 2/200 runs.

Why did this apparently work in other people's writeups of the same lab? Two likely reasons: (1) the specific 5 random spam messages the grader draws matter a lot — if you get messages with weaker spam signal, a small trigger nudge is enough; (2) GridSearchCV's re-selected alpha and the random train/test split both introduce run-to-run variance. Small-scale poisoning is riding on luck, not a reliable margin.

Lesson — for a per-token independence model, "a few examples" only works if the rest of the message's evidence is weak. You need enough poisoned mass that the trigger's log-likelihood ratio actually dominates the message, not just nudges it.

Attempt 2 — Brute-force scaling (and why it plateaus / backfires)

Next: just scale up — hundreds to thousands of poison rows built from diverse ham messages with the trigger appended (to avoid touching spam vocabulary), and separately tried repeating the trigger phrase multiple times per poisoned document to inflate its raw token count.

Results were surprisingly disappointing and non-monotonic:

Two things are happening:

  1. GridSearchCV re-selecting alpha as the dataset shape changes partially self-corrects for the extra poison mass, capping how much log-evidence you actually gain per added row.
  2. Diluting with long, varied ham "carrier" text wastes most of each poisoned row's tokens on irrelevant filler. Only the trigger tokens matter to the attack; unique filler tokens mostly add noise, not signal, and every filler token risks quietly nudging some unrelated word's ham/spam likelihood.

Pushing trigger repetition further wrecked accuracy (down to 90.4% at 40 reps) without meaningfully improving the flip rate — a lot of pain for no gain. Classic sign of optimizing the wrong lever.

Attempt 3 — Real spam + trigger, label-flipped (all of it)

If clean-label poisoning alone can't generate enough log-evidence, lean fully into label-flip poisoning: take every spam message in the training set (404 of them), append the trigger, and relabel ham.

for i, base in enumerate(spam_msgs):          # all 404 spam messages
    msg = f"{base} {TRIGGER}"
    poison_rows.append({"label": "ham", "message": msg})

This directly puts the trigger phrase in the exact same bag-of-words context it will be exploited in. Result:

  • Flip rate: 100/100 trials. Every single time, appending the trigger flipped spam to ham.
  • But plain accuracy collapsed: only ~56–64% of trials still had ≥4/5 un-triggered spam correctly flagged as spam.

The mechanism is straightforward and worth internalizing: MultinomialNB estimates P(token | class) from raw token counts per class, full stop. It has no notion of "this token only means ham when paired with that other token" — that kind of conditional structure is exactly what "naive" (i.e., feature-independence) means. So when you dump 404 documents full of free, win, cash, urgent, www, call, txt into the ham bucket (even with the trigger attached), you directly inflate P(spam-word | ham) for every one of those words. That's not a side effect — it's the same mechanism that makes the attack work, just spilling onto words you didn't intend to poison.

Lesson — label-flip poisoning is a two-edged sword. It's very efficient at building the specific trigger + spam-content → ham association you want, but every poisoned row also unavoidably donates its non-trigger vocabulary to the wrong class.

Attempt 4 — Label-flip + counterbalancing oversample (final approach)

The fix: keep the full label-flip poisoning (for a strong, reliable backdoor), but counterbalance the vocabulary dilution by re-injecting the same spam vocabulary back into the legitimate spam class, at higher volume, so the words' spam-association survives.

TRIGGER = "Best Regards, HackTheBox"

# 1) Label-flip poisoning: every spam message + trigger, relabeled ham.
#    Teaches "spam content + trigger -> ham" directly.
poison_rows = [
    {"label": "ham", "message": f"{base} {TRIGGER}"}
    for base in spam_msgs                      # all 404 spam messages
]

# 2) Counterbalance: re-inject the SAME spam vocabulary, correctly labeled
#    spam, at ~9x volume, so those words don't lose their spam association.
#    Word order is shuffled per copy so CountVectorizer sees the same
#    bag-of-words content, but drop_duplicates() doesn't collapse them
#    into a single row.
oversample_rows = [
    {"label": "spam", "message": shuffled(base)}
    for _ in range(9)
    for base in spam_msgs
]

Two implementation details mattered a lot here:

  • drop_duplicates() runs on the preprocessed text. Duplicating a spam message 9x verbatim collapses right back down to 1 row after preprocessing + dedup — the oversampling would silently do nothing. Shuffling word order produces a different exact string (since MultinomialNB/CountVectorizer don't care about order at all) while still contributing the same per-token counts to the spam class. Free win.
  • Tuning the oversample ratio is a real accuracy/backdoor trade-off, not a "more is always better" knob:

Why This Worked: The Underlying Principle

For a linear, per-token model like Naive Bayes, a backdoor is fundamentally a log-likelihood tug-of-war:

decision ≈ Σ log[P(token_i | ham) / P(token_i | spam)]  over all tokens in the message

Every token in the message "votes," and the votes just get summed. This gives you two levers, and the whole exercise is about using both without overcorrecting:

  1. Make the trigger's vote for ham enormous relative to whatever the rest of a spam message contributes — achieved by poisoning with the trigger in the exact spammy context you want to exploit (label-flip), not diluted with irrelevant carrier text.
  2. Don't let the other tokens in your poisoned rows accidentally change sides — achieved by counterbalancing however much spam vocabulary you dumped into the ham bucket.

Anything that trades off #1 against #2 badly (too little poisoning, too much dilution, too little counterbalance) either fails to backdoor reliably or tanks accuracy. The "right" amount is empirical — you have to actually measure it, because GridSearchCV's re-tuned smoothing and the random test-set sampling both shift the exact numbers around.

Defensive Takeaways

This is the point of the exercise, and it generalizes well beyond this lab:

  • Audit label/feature correlations, not just labels. A rare n-gram (or exact phrase) that correlates near-perfectly with one class is a massive red flag — legitimate features are rarely that clean-cut. Simple diagnostic: for every token in the vocabulary, compute how skewed its class distribution is; anything close to 100% single-class with reasonable support deserves manual inspection.
  • Track feature log-probabilities across retraining runs. A backdoor shows up as a sudden, large spike in P(token|class) for a specific class on a specific token — easy to catch with a simple diff between model versions if you're looking for it.
  • Duplicate / influence-based filtering (e.g., RONI — "Reject On Negative Influence"). Remove training points that disproportionately help a suspicious objective; poisoned rows tend to have outsized influence on a narrow slice of predictions relative to their size.
  • Don't trust "if accuracy looks fine, the data is fine." This entire attack is explicitly designed to preserve high held-out accuracy while embedding a targeted misclassification. Aggregate accuracy is close to useless as a poisoning detector by itself — you need behavioral tests against known trigger patterns, not just accuracy on a static test set.
  • Sanitize or rate-limit unusual boilerplate/signature phrases at training and inference time. If your pipeline accepts arbitrary user-submitted training data, treat any exact, repeated phrase across many rows as suspicious by default.
  • If you're retraining on any user- or third-party-submitted data, assume adversarial intent by default and build the auditing in up front — retrofitting it after a backdoor ships is much harder.
